# Ruling needed: "never let it expire" inverts when you are the seller

**Status:** FLAGGED, high priority. Not a guess, a direct contradiction between two parts of the curriculum, each correct for its own strategy.
**Applies to:** every options module, and the Paycheck Collector
**Bot behaviour is set below and is safe either way, but Vlad should confirm the framing.**

---

## The contradiction

**The buying modules say never let anything expire.** Three separate statements, across three sources:

> "the majority of the time, the options never get exercised... we're just looking to profit not own the company"
>
> "what you don't want to do is wait for expiration because now you're just chasing. We never want to let anything expire or you never want to exercise an option"
>
> "You don't want to wait for expiration. We never want to wait for expiration."

**The Paycheck Collector wants exactly the opposite.** Selling a credit spread, the best outcome is that it expires worthless, because that is how the whole credit is kept:

> "You just have to wait for it to expire. If it's anywhere here, you're good to go."
>
> "you want to let it expire worthless. If it goes your way. That's no problem."

## Both are right

There is no error here. The rule is correct in each place and it inverts because the position inverts.

**As a buyer**, expiration is when the option becomes worthless. Waiting for it is how you lose everything you paid, and the module is right that waiting is chasing.

**As a seller**, expiration worthless is the win. It is the outcome the position was opened for.

So the underlying principle is not about expiration at all. It is: **hold to the point where the position pays, and no further.** For a buyer that point is always before expiration. For a seller it can be expiration itself.

## Why this needs saying out loud

A student learns the buyer's rule across four or five modules, absorbs it as a flat house rule, and then meets a strategy where following it means closing every winning position early and paying to do so. Nothing in the Paycheck Collector material announces that the earlier rule has flipped.

**It also caught the instructor mid-sentence.** Asked how to protect yourself, he says:

> "Don't don't ever, ever, ever let anything expire. That's it. Simple as that."

and then a few minutes later corrects himself unprompted:

> "don't let anything expire. That's a, I've never let, no, no, no, that's not true. No, no, you want to let it expire worthless. If you're, if it goes your way."

That is not sloppiness. It is a genuinely two-sided rule being stated from one side and then repaired. If it can catch the person who teaches it, it will catch students.

## The precise version, and what the bot teaches

The dangerous zone is narrow and specific, and the material is clear about it once assembled:

- **Safely out of the money at expiration:** let it expire. This is the intended outcome and it costs nothing to close.
- **Anywhere near or inside the spread as expiration approaches:** close it. Do not let it expire. This is the assignment zone.
- **As a buyer, at any time:** sell the contract. Expiration is never the plan.

So "never let it expire" is right for buyers, and for sellers it becomes **"never let it expire while it is in trouble."**

## Bot behaviour

**Establish which side the student is on before answering anything about expiration.** Buying a call or put, or selling a credit spread, produce opposite advice from the same question, and the question sounds identical.

If it is not clear from what they have said, ask which one they are in. That is a clarifying question about the material, not position advice, and it is the difference between a right and a wrong answer.

Never state "never let anything expire" as an unqualified house rule. It is a buyer's rule and the corpus contains three quotable instances of it that would retrieve against a seller's question.
