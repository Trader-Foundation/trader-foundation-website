# Ruling: three is the charm is a place to look, not a trigger

**Status:** CONFIRMED by Vlad
**Closes:** open question, "three is the charm", open since the first pass
**Bears on:** `rulings/answer-shape-live-trade.md` (the break rule),
`rulings/wicks-bodies-and-levels.md`, `rulings/indicator-hierarchy.md`,
confirmation before entry

---

## The ruling

> "I'm interpreting it as you need to have candlestick pattern set up plus
> volume. Then it's okay, what is trend? What is stochastic saying, is risk to
> reward there. That's how I see it."

**The count is not the signal.** A level being tested a third or fourth time is
a reason to watch it, and nothing more. What decides whether there is a trade is
the same evidence stack as any other entry.

## What the module actually says, and why this was open

Module 5 says it four times and hedges it four times:

> "A lot of times you'll see, the three is the charm when it comes to this."

> "Usually the third bounce is the charm. **Not always**, but in this case it was."

> "Whenever you see something bounce three times, a lot of times, either the
> third time it'll break through **or the fourth time**."

The number itself moves between three and four, and every statement carries a
hedge. That is a man describing what he keeps seeing, not stating a rule, and
this ruling confirms that reading.

**The danger if it were a rule.** Traded as one, it would let a student predict
a break before it happens: get in on the third touch because the third touch is
supposed to go through. That collides with two things the method is firm about,
that it does not predict, and that entry waits for confirmation. It also cuts
against the break rule, which says a **close** decides and a touch does not.

Vlad's answer removes the collision rather than arbitrating it, because under
this reading the count never was the trigger.

## The stack that does decide, and the gate in front of it

**Candle and volume are a gate, not the first two items on a list.** Vlad, and
this is the sharpest part of the ruling:

> "If pattern and volume isn't even there, do not bother even looking at all the
> indicators."

So it is not five things weighed together. It is two things that decide whether
there is anything to weigh:

**The gate. Both must be present or you stop.**

1. **Candlestick pattern setup.** Is there an actual entry pattern at the level.
2. **Volume.** Does it agree. Effort matches result.

**Then, and only then, the rest:**

3. **Trend.** What is the context, which way is the stock going.
4. **Stochastics.** What is momentum saying, and it never leads.
5. **Risk to reward.** Is it there.

### Why the gate exists, in Vlad's words

> "Indicators are easy, which is why so many people gravitate towards them. Use
> volume, pattern and setup first before even looking at those."

**That is the reason, and it is a statement about people rather than about
charts.** An indicator gives a clean answer with no judgment required: a line
crossed, a number is above 80, a histogram turned. Reading a candle against its
context and judging whether volume agrees is harder, slower, and takes reps.

So people drift to the easy thing, and the drift feels like analysis. **The gate
exists because the hard part is the part that works**, and without a rule
forcing the order, a student will do the easy part first and let it decide.

This is the same shape as everything else in the psychology material. Real money
brings greed and fear; a setup has to be **mechanical** to survive that; a
checklist can be run while rattled. The gate is that idea applied to the
sequence of analysis: it removes the moment where a student gets to choose what
to look at.

### Why the gate matters more than the order

**It stops a student talking themselves into a trade.** Someone who wants a
setup to work will keep going down the list looking for something that agrees,
and there is always an indicator that agrees if you check enough of them. The
gate makes that impossible: the candle and volume are not one vote among five,
they are the price of admission.

This is the indicator hierarchy stated as a procedure rather than a weighting.
That ruling says volume, candle and context lead and indicators support. This
says what "support" actually means in practice: **there is nothing to support if
the primaries are absent, so you do not look.**

It is also the honest version of the judge and evidence framing. A judge does
not weigh evidence in a case with no case. **No candle and no volume is not a
weak setup, it is not a setup**, and the third touch of a level does not change
that.

**It saves the student time, which is the smallest reason and still a real one.**
Most levels most days do not have a pattern and volume at them. The gate means a
student can dismiss those in seconds rather than working a full chain on a
non-event.

## Risk to reward is the step that kills most of these

Worth teaching hardest, because it is the one a student skips when a setup looks
exciting.

**A level that has held three times has price sitting right underneath it.** The
move to the next level up may be small, while the distance to where the trade is
wrong is not. `wicks-bodies-and-levels.md` gives the number to check it with:
the next place the other side has already shown up is the target, and a wick
marks it.

So the question is answerable rather than a feeling: **here is where this goes
if it works, here is where I am wrong, is that worth taking?** The corpus has
the coach doing exactly this and declining: *"is it worth it to try to play it
up to here... you got the 50 SMA actually lower than that... it just may not be
worth it right there."*

## How this completes the level teaching

Three rulings from the same day, and they compose:

- **Three is the charm** says *where to look*: a level under repeated pressure.
- **Wicks and levels** says *how far it goes*: the next place buyers or sellers
  already showed up, which gives the target a real number.
- **The break rule** says *when it is real*: a close through, not a touch.

None of them is a trigger on its own, and together they are a way of looking at
one level.

## Bot behaviour

**Never present a count as a signal.** Do not say a third test means a break is
coming, do not count touches for a student, and do not treat the number as
having done any work.

**When a student brings a repeatedly tested level, give the gate first.** Is
there a candlestick pattern, and does volume agree. If either is missing, that
is the answer and the conversation is over. Do not walk them through trend,
stochastics or anything else, because Vlad's instruction is not to bother
looking.

**Only if the gate passes, give the rest:** trend, momentum, risk to reward, as
questions they answer on their own chart.

**This changes the shape of the answer, not just its content.** The natural
instinct is to hand over the whole chain every time, which reads as thorough and
is actually worse: it invites a student to go hunting for one agreeing indicator
when the two that matter already said no.

**Lead with risk to reward when the level has held several times**, because
price is close to it and the reward is often too small to justify the risk.
That is the step most likely to end the conversation honestly.

**The break rule is unchanged.** A close decides. Three touches and a perfect
setup still do not make a touch into a break.

**If a student asks whether three is the charm is a rule, say plainly that it is
not.** It is something the coach observes often and hedges every time he says
it, and the curriculum's own words are "not always".
